3.1Sequences. Convergence

So far we have had quite a limited amount of operations that we are allowed to do with complex numbers. It is time to introduce the basics of analysis into the picture, which for us means that we will deal with the convergent sequences of complex numbers, and hence with limits. It is certainly tempting to assume that the students already know this material from an introductory real analysis course, but in many situations it is not so. Everyone, however, saw at least parts of this material in Calculus I or II, and I will assume that a number of facts can be stated without proofs (to be honest, these facts are almost never proved in Calculus I or II, and another temptation is to diligently work through sometimes tedious and boring epsilon–delta arguments, but choosing this path will delay the key facts of the complex analysis per se, so instead we will move rather fast and yet try not to skip anything important).

We had a short discussion about the real numbers \(\R\) — recall that the set \(\R\) is an ordered field. What else can be said about \(\R\)? For instance, as the student should be able to check without much work, the set \(\Q\) of rational numbers is also an ordered field. There must be some key difference. Indeed, this difference comes from analysis, or, more specifically, when we start making some limiting arguments. In short, the set \(\R\), opposite to \(\Q\), “has no holes,” which will be explained mathematically later.

First, let me introduce what we need to perform some sort of limiting arguments. Actually, the only thing, which is needed, is an ability to measure the closeness of the elements of the set. In other words, for all elements of the given set we must have a function, which is usually called distance or metric, that satisfies basic natural properties such that it is always non-negative, equal to zero if one measures the distance from any element to itself, commutative, and satisfies the triangle inequality (the distance between any two elements is not bigger than the sum of the distances of these elements to an arbitrary third element). Since in both \(\R\) and \(\C\) we have the absolute value function that, as we discussed, measures the distance from the origin, we immediately have (I will write the definition for the set of complex numbers, but for the real numbers it is verbatim the same)

Proposition 3.1. Let \(d\colon \C\times \C\longrightarrow \R\) be defined by \(d(z,w)=|z-w|\) for all \(z,w\in\C\). Then

  1. \(d(z,z)=0\);

  2. \(d(z,w)>0\) if \(z\neq w\);

  3. \(d(z,w)=d(w,z)\);

  4. \(d(z,w)\leq d(z,v)+d(w,v)\),

i.e., in words, \(d(z,w)=|z-w|\) is a metric on the set \(\C\).

Proof. The first three properties are immediate. The last one follows from the triangle inequality for the modulus of complex number. Indeed, \begin{align*} d(z,w)&=|z-w|=\\ &=|z-v+v-w|\leq\\ &\leq|z-v|+|v-w|=\\ &=d(z,v)+d(w,v). \end{align*} ■

The sets with distance function are called metric spaces and hence I just proved that \(\C\) is a metric space.

Example 3.2. Let me use the ability to measure distances to prove a geometric fact. Assume that three circles with the centers at \(O_1,O_2,O_3\) of the same radius intersect at the same point \(O\). Let \(A_1,A_2,A_3\) be the other points of their intersections. The goal is to show that the triangles \(O_1O_2O_3\) and \(A_1A_2A_3\) are equal.

Here is an immediate observation, if I put the origin at the point \(O\) and denote the coordinates of the centers as \(z_1,z_2,z_3\), then the second point \(A_1\) of intersection of circles with centers \(O_1,O_2\) can be found as \(z_1+z_2\), the second point \(A_2\) of intersection of circles with centers \(O_1,O_3\) is \(z_1+z_3\), and the second point \(A_3\) of intersection of circles with centers \(O_2,O_3\) is \(z_2+z_3\) (make a sketch if something is not clear here). Now I have, for instance, that \(|A_1A_2|=|z_1+z_2-z_1-z_3|=|z_2-z_3|=|O_2O_3|\), similarly I’ll find that two other sides are equal to \(O_1O_3\) and \(O_1O_2\). Hence the triangles are equal.

Now to the limits. I say that a sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) of complex (or real) numbers is given if for any \(k=1,2,\ldots\) it is possible (at least in principle) to compute complex number \(z_k\).

Definition 3.3. Complex (or real) number \(z\) is called the limit of the sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) if no matter how small real number \(\varepsilon>0\) is chosen, it is always possible to find a natural number \(n\in\N\), such that for all natural \(k> n\) it is true that \[ d(z_k,z)=|z_k-z|<\varepsilon. \] If \(z\) is a limit of \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) then we write \[ \lim_{k\to\infty}z_k=z, \] or \[ z_k\to z,\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty. \]

Definition 3.4. The sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) is called bounded if there exists number \(M\) such that \(|z_k|<M\) for all \(k=1,2,\ldots\). If sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) is not bounded it is called unbounded.

Example 3.5. Let me convince you that the limit of the sequence of real numbers \((r^k)_{k=1}^\infty\) such that \(r\geq 0\) is equal to 0 if \(0\leq r<1\), to \(1\) if \(r=1\) and the sequence is unbounded otherwise. If \(r=0\) all the elements of the sequence are zeros, and conclusion follows. If \(r=1\) all the elements of the sequence are equal to 1, and again, using the fact that \(|r^k-1|=|1^k-1|=|1-1|=0<\varepsilon\), the limit is 1.

If \(0<r<1\), consider the inequality \[ |r^k-0|=r^k<\varepsilon \] for an arbitrary \(\varepsilon>0\). Using the properties of the natural \(\log\), I have \[ k\log r<\log\varepsilon\implies k>\frac{\log \varepsilon}{\log r}\,, \] where I changed the sign of the inequality since \(\log r<0\) by the assumption. Finally, choosing \(n\) as the first positive integer such that \[ n\geq \frac{\log \varepsilon}{\log r}\,, \] I get that for all \(k>n\) I have \[ r^k<\varepsilon, \] hence the limit is zero.

Now assume \(r>1\) and take an arbitrary large \(M>0\). By the same properties of the natural logarithm I will find that if \(k>\frac{\log M}{\log r}\) then \(r^k>M\), and since \(M\) was arbitrary, the sequence is unbounded.

Exercise 1. Consider the omitted cases \(r<0\).

Exercise 2. Analyze the sequence \(\left(\frac{1}{k^p}\right)_{k=1}^\infty\) for some \(p>0\).

Here are a few properties of convergent sequences, whose proofs are multiple application of the given definition. As explained above, I will not spend my time on showing all the details. The students are invited to practice their proof skills.

Proposition 3.6. Let \(a_k\to a\) and \(b_k\to b\). Then

  1. Both \((a_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) and \((b_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) are bounded (convergent sequences are bounded);

  2. The limit of a convergent sequence is unique;

  3. \(a_k\pm b_k\to a\pm b\);

  4. \(a_kb_k\to ab\);

  5. If, additionally, \(b_k\neq 0\) and \(b\neq 0\) then \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}\to \frac{a}{b}\).

Ok, we have a definition and some basic properties. One of the key questions usually is whether the given sequence converges. To use the definition above, one needs to know the value of the limit to apply it, but in many cases we have no such information. Here is the way out of this situation.

Definition 3.7. The sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) is called fundamental or Cauchy, if no matter how small real number \(\varepsilon>0\) is chosen, there is a natural number \(n\) such that for all natural \(k,m>n\) simultaneously it is true that \[ d(z_k,z_m)=|z_k-z_m|<\varepsilon. \]

Exercise 3. Yet another proof exercise: show that if a sequence has the limit then it is fundamental.

And here comes the key fact that distinguishes, e.g., \(\R\) from \(\Q\). I will announce it as an axiom, but depending on how you develop the properties of real numbers, it can be given a proof based on other equivalent axioms or on the way one constructs real numbers. The full discussion is certainly outside of the scope of the current course.

Completeness axiom of \(\R\): Any fundamental sequence \((x_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) of real numbers has the limit \(x\in \R\). Equivalently, we say that set \(\R\) is complete.

Example 3.8. The set \(\Q\) with the same metric \(d(x,y)=|x-y|\) is not complete. Take, for instance, the sequence \(x_1=1,x_2=1.4,x_3=1.41,x_4=1.414,x_5=1.4142,\ldots\). Here the \(k\)-th element is the \(k\)-th rational approximation of \(\sqrt{2}\). Any element of this sequence is a rational number. It is clearly fundamental (think this out), but its limit is not a rational number. Rational numbers have holes, real numbers do not. To do analysis, one needs a complete set, and this is why we do analysis on \(\R\) and not \(\Q\).

Can we do analysis on \(\C\)? Certainly, yes! To conclude that \(\C\) is complete, I will state

Proposition 3.9. Let \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) be a sequence of complex numbers. Let \(z_k=x_k+\I y_k\), i.e., \((x_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) and \((y_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) are the sequences of real numbers. Then \(z_k\to z=x+\I y\) if and only if \(x_k\to x\) and \(y_k\to y\).

Exercise 4. Prove Proposition 3.9. Use it to prove that the set \(\C\) is complete, i.e., prove that \(z_k\to z\) if and only if \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) is fundamental.

So far the difference between the real and complex cases has been minimal, and all the proofs are, in fact, identical, since they all rely on the identical properties of the distance function. Here is one important case, where there is an important distinction. In the real line one has two infinities: \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\). In addition to the sequences that converge to a number, one can also consider sequences that converge to \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\). In the complex case the infinity is unique! More precisely, it is said that the sequence of complex (not real!) numbers \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) converges to infinity, written \[ \lim_{k\to\infty}z_k=\infty, \] if for any \(M>0\) there exists \(n\in\N\) such that \(|z_k|>M\) for all \(k>n\) (in other words, \(|z_k|\to\infty\)).

Hence, for instance, in the real analysis the sequence defined by \(x_k=(-2)^k\) has no limit (even if we allow \(\pm\infty\) as our limits), whereas in the complex analysis the sequence defined by \(z_k=(-2)^k\) has the infinite limit, and we write \[ \lim_{k\to\infty}z_k=\infty. \] (But remember, each complex number is a pair of real numbers, so, strictly speaking, we have the sequence of points with coordinates \((x_k,y_k)=\bigl((-2)^k,0\bigr)\), which will be important for us later.)

Example 3.10. Now consider the most important for us example: the sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\) with \[ z_k=w^k,\quad w\in \C, \] which is a generalization of Example 3.5 to the complex plane.

First consider the simple cases \(|w|\neq 1\). I have, since \(|w^k|=|w|^k\) by the properties of the modulus, if \(|w|<1\) then \(z_k\to 0\) for the same reason as in Example 3.5. If \(|w|>1\) then, again using Example 3.5, \(|z_k|=|w|^k>M\) for all sufficiently large \(k\), and hence I write \(z_k\to \infty\). If \(w=1\) I have \(z_k\to 1\).

Finally, if \(|w|=1\) and \(w\neq 1\) then the limit does not exist. Indeed, in this case \(|w^k|=|w|^k=1\), and now I need to look at the arguments \(\Arg z_k=\Arg w^k=k \Arg w\) (and by the assumption \(\Arg w\neq 2\pi n\) for any \(n\in\Z\)), the points of my sequence will be traveling with the same step along the unit circle without approaching any limit. It is possible to have two quite different cases. In Case 1, \(\Arg w\) is commensurate with \(2\pi\), say \(\Arg w=2\pi\frac{p}{q}\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime. Then all \(k\arg w\) numbers are different if \(k=1,\ldots,q\) but \(w^{q+1}=w,\,w^{q+2}=w^2\), etc. Whence I have \(q\) limit points of the sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty\), namely \(w,w^2,\ldots, w^q\). If, in Case 2, \(\Arg w\) is not commensurate with \(2\pi\) then any point on the unit circle will be a limit point of my sequence, but I will skip the rigorous mathematical arguments.

3.2Regions

Another thing, which can be defined by using the metric function \(d\), is the open sets. First, I will define a neighborhood of point \(w\) of radius \(\varepsilon>0\), which I denote \(B_\varepsilon(w)\), as the set of points, whose distance to \(w\) is less than \(\varepsilon\): \[ B_\varepsilon(w)=\{z\in\C\colon d(z,w)=|z-w|<\varepsilon\}. \] The condition \(|z-w|<\varepsilon\) literally means that all the points \(z\) belong to the disk \(B_\varepsilon(w)\) with the center \(w\) and radius \(\varepsilon\), see Fig. 1. (\(B\) in the notation stands for “balls,” which is the usual notation, especially when one moves to general \(n\)-dimensional space; I will also use \(B_\varepsilon\) to denote \(B_\varepsilon(0)\), i.e., if the center of my disk is the origin.)

Definition 3.11. A set \(E\subseteq \C\), which is a subset of complex plane \(\C\), is called open, if for any point \(z\in E\) there exists a neighborhood \(B_\varepsilon(z)\), which belongs to \(E\), i.e., \(B_\varepsilon(z)\subseteq E\).

In other words, any point of an open set is an interior point (I will not go into carefully defining all the topological terms since we simply will not need it, for us in most situations our usual intuition about the terms such as “interior” will be enough).

First, let me prove

Proposition 3.12. \(B_r(w)\) is an open set.

Proof. Let \(B_r(w)\) be fixed and take any \(a\in B_r(w)\). By the definition of neighborhood I have \(|w-a|<r\), therefore, \[ \varepsilon=\frac{r-|w-a|}{2}>0 \] is well defined.

I claim that \(B_\varepsilon(a)\subseteq B_r(w)\). Indeed, take an arbitrary \(z\in B_{\varepsilon}(a)\) and estimate: \[ |z-w|=|z-a+a-w|\leq |z-a|+|w-a|<\varepsilon+|w-a|=\frac{r+|w-a|}{2}<\frac{2r}{2}=r \] as required. ■

Exercise 5. Show that the set \(E_r(w)=\{z\in\C\colon |z-w|>r\}\) is open.

Here is an example of another open set.

Example 3.13. What set on the plane \(\R^2\) is defined by the condition \[ \Im z^2>2? \] I have \(z^2=(x+\I y)^2=(x^2-y^2)+\I 2xy\), and therefore \(\Im z^2=2 xy>2\) or, finally, \(xy>1\), which is the set of points above and under the hyperbola \(xy=1\), see Fig. 2.

Since now we have open sets, we can also define closed sets, which are usually defined as the sets, complements to which are open. This is a general abstract topological definition but not an easy one to work with. So, instead (in the spirit of analysis), I will define a closed set using the notion of convergent sequence, which we discussed above. I will leave it as an exercise to show that the two definitions are equivalent.

Definition 3.14. A set \(F\subseteq \C\) is called closed if for all possible convergent sequences \(z_k\to z\), where all \(z_k\in F\), it follows that \(z\in F\) as well.

In other words, a set \(F\) is closed if it is closed under the operation of taking limits.

Here is the main example.

Proposition 3.15. The set \[ \bar{B}_r(w)=\{z\in\C\colon |z-w|\leq r\} \] is closed.

Proof. Let \(z_k\in \bar{B}_r(w)\) and assume that \(z_k\to z\). I need to show that \(z\in \bar B_r(w)\). Indeed, \[ |z-w|=|z-z_k+z_k-w|\leq |z-z_k|+|z_k-w|\leq |z-z_k|+r. \] Since \(|z-z_k|\to 0\) as \(k\to\infty\) and the left-hand side does not depend on \(k\), I get \(|z-w|\leq r\), as needed. ■

Here is another example.

Example 3.16. What set of points on the complex plane is defined by \[ -\frac \pi 2\leq \arg(z+1-\I)\leq \frac{3\pi}{4}\,? \] The complex number \(z+1-\I=z-(-1+\I)\) geometrically is a vector, going from the point \(-1+\I\) to the point \(z\). This vector must have the principal argument between \(-\pi/2\) and \(3\pi/4\), and hence I get the set, which is shown in Fig. 3 (the vertex \(-1+\I\) itself, where the argument is undefined, must be added to make this set closed).

A little more terminology. A set \(E\) is said to be bounded if there exists an \(r>0\) such that \(|z|<r\) for all points \(z\in E\) (in other words, set \(E\) is bounded if there exists \(B_r\) such that \(E\subseteq B_r\)). A closed and bounded set \(E\subseteq\C\) is called compact. The boundary \(\partial E\) of an open set \(E\), is the set of points \(z\in\C,\,z\notin E\) such that for each such point there exists a sequence \((z_k)_{k=1}^\infty,\,z_k\in E\), and \(z_k\to z\). For us the boundaries will be continuous curves, see below. The closure of an open set \(E\), which I will denote \(\bar{E}\), is thus \(\bar E=E\cup \partial E\), and \(\bar{E}\) is, of course, closed.

In addition to the notion of open and closed sets we will need a very basic understanding of connected sets. Specifically, set \(E\) is called connected if all the pairs of its points \(z,w\in E\) can be connected by a continuous curve, all points of which are also in \(E\). To be more precise, I assume existence of a parametric curve \[ \sigma(t)=\varphi(t)+\I\psi(t)\quad t_1\leq t\leq t_2, \] such that \(\varphi,\psi\) are real continuous functions of variable \(t\), \(z=\varphi(t_1)+\I \psi(t_1),w=\varphi(t_2)+\I \psi(t_2)\), and for any \(t\) satisfying \(t_1\leq t\leq t_2\), \(\sigma(t)\in E\). The set in Example 3.13 is not connected whereas the set in Example 3.16 is connected.

I already mentioned an analytical description of a curve on \(\C\), it is a pair of continuous real functions. We will talk much more about specific requirements for these curves later. For now, let me just say that if the end of a curve coincides with its beginning, it is quite naturally called closed; if a curve has no points of self-intersection other than, possibly, its beginning and end, then it is called simple. A connected set \(E\subseteq \C\) is called simply connected if for any simple closed curve \(\gamma\) that belongs to this set (\(\gamma\subseteq E\)), the set of all complex points that are inside \(\gamma\) also belongs to \(E\) (the set “has no holes”). The set is called multi connected if it is not simply connected (see Fig. 4).

And here is the time to introduce the definition I was after this whole lecture.

Definition 3.17. A domain or region \(E\subseteq \C\) is a non-empty open connected set.

Optional material

*3.3Jordan’s curve theorem

I mentioned above that in our class we will mostly skip the mathematical technicalities of various topological concepts such as connectedness, being interior or exterior, etc. An honest treatment of the most general case is not a simple affair. Just to give you an idea of potential difficulties (which are not obvious initially), I would like to mention the famous theorem of Camille Jordan (formulated in 1887), which states (using the terminology introduced above) that any simple closed curve divides the plane into two connected components: one is interior, which is bounded, and another one is exterior, which is unbounded. The statement is so “obvious” that it seems, at least initially, that there is nothing to prove here. And yet, after understanding that continuous curves on the plane can be extremely wild, the question about rigorous arguments that would provide a proof arises. Just to give you an indication of possible complexity, I invite you to pick a point in Fig. 5 and decide for yourself whether this point is inside or outside of a simple closed curve, which is present there (trust me, there is one and only one closed curve in this figure). And yes, the proof is not simple, because in the most general case we cannot assume that the curve we are dealing with has a tangent vector at almost all the points. For applications, however, such generality is seldom necessary, and it is safe to assume that the curves we are dealing with, are smooth (again, maybe with an exception of a few special points, to allow us to have squares and rectangles as our domains). The mathematical details of the curves that will be acceptable for us will be discussed later, but for now I want the students to have at least a vague idea that a lot of intricate mathematics is left out.
