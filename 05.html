In the previous lecture I introduced the central notion of our course: holomorphic functions. Recall,

Definition 5.1. Let \(E\subseteq \C\) be an open non-empty set, and let \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\). Then \(f\) is called holomorphic on \(E\) if it is differentiable at every point \(z\in E\).

Why so much emphasis? Because, as I will try to convince you, being holomorphic is much more restrictive than just request that both real and imaginary parts of \(f\) are (real) differentiable. For instance, why the discussion about continuity was so short? Because it is true that

Proposition 5.2. Let \(E\) be an open set. Then \(f(z)=f(x+\I y)=u(x,y)+\I v(x,y)\) is continuous on \(E\) if and only if \(u\) and \(v\) are both continuous on \(E\) as real functions of two real variables.

Exercise 1. Prove this proposition.

So, in some sense, while moving into the complex world, we do not gain anything with respect to the notion of continuity compared with our familiar real world of \(\R^2\). It is very different with differentiability!

5.1Cauchy–Riemann equations

It is reasonable to expect, of course, that complex differentiability of \(f\) must be connected somehow with differentiability of two real valued functions \(u,v\), which we find “inside” our complex function: \[ f(z)=f(x+\I y)=u(x,y)+\I v(x,y). \] Let me be very precise here. Recall that differentiability of real functions of several variables is related to existence of its partial derivatives. I will use the usual notation \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y)=u'_x(x,y)=u_x(x,y), \] and very often I will write \(u'_x\) or \(u_x\) if the points at which the partial derivatives are evaluated are clear. Recall that, for instance, by definition \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{x\to x_0}\frac{u(x,y_0)-u(x_0,y_0)}{x-x_0}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{u(x_0+h,y_0)-u(x_0,y_0)}{h}\,, \] i.e., it is the usual derivative of function \(u\) with respect to variable \(x\) with the assumption that the second variable is held constant. Similar for the second partial derivative.

This is what one can get almost immediately:

Proposition 5.3. Assume that \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) is differentiable at \(E\ni z_0=x_0+\I y_0\). Then at this point \((x_0,y_0)\) there exist partial derivatives \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\) and, moreover, they satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations \begin{equation}\label{eq5:1} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x_0,y_0)=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x_0,y_0)\,,\quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x_0,y_0)=-\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x_0,y_0)\,. \end{equation}

Proof. First I need to show that these partial derivatives exist. By my assumption I have \[ \frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}\to f'(z_0) \] for any \(h\) that approaches \(0\) and hence for real ones. For real \(h\), in more details, \[ \frac{u(x_0+h,y_0)-u(x_0,y_0)}{h}+\I \frac{v(x_0+h,y_0)-v(x_0,y_0)}{h}\to f'(z_0). \] Since this limit exists, it implies that for both real and imaginary parts the corresponding limits exist (we had this exercise before) but these limits by definition are exactly \(u_x\) and \(v_x\) at \((x_0,y_0)\) and I proved the existence of partial derivatives with respect to \(x\). Similarly, assuming that \(h\) is purely imaginary, I can show that both \(u_y\) and \(v_y\) exist (fill in the details).

Now I will differentiate \(f\) with respect to \(x\) using the chain rule: \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(z_0)=f'(z_0)\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=f'(z_0). \] From direct differentiation I also get that \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(z_0)=u_x(x_0,y_0)+\I v_x(x_0,y_0). \] Similarly, \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(z_0)=f'(z_0)\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=\I f'(z_0)=u_y(x_0,y_0)+\I v_y(x_0,y_0). \] After multiplying the last equality by \(-\I\) I must conclude that \[u_x+\I v_x=v_y-\I u_y, \] at \((x_0,y_0)\) or \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) as required. ■

It would be great to have the converse statement, but in general the converse is not true (see the homework problems), i.e., the existence of partial derivatives that satisfy \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) is not enough to conclude the differentiability of \(f\). Something else is required. It can be proved (I will skip a proof here since it is more about real differentiability) that

Proposition 5.4. Assume that \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\) exist, satisfy \(\eqref{eq5:1}\), and continuous at \(z_0\). Then \(f=u+\I v\) is differentiable at \(z_0\).

For a little headache: the converse statement to this proposition is also false in general! So it is only a sufficient condition for differentiability at a point.

To show that holomorphic functions are “nice,” I state one more result, the proof of which will be given later. Recall that now I am talking about differentiability on a set, not at a single point.

Proposition 5.5. Let \(E\subseteq \C\) be an open set, and \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\). Then \(f\) is holomorphic on \(E\) if and only if \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\) exist, continuous, and satisfy \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) on \(E\).

Example 5.6. We already know that \(f(z)=z^2\) is entire. Not surprisingly, for \[ u(x,y)=x^2-y^2,\quad v(x,y)=2xy, \] \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) hold at every point of \(\C\).

Example 5.7. Let \(f(z)=\bar z^2\). Then \[ u(x,y)=x^2-y^2,\quad v(x,y)=-2 xy, \] and \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) hold only at \(x=y=0\). Hence I can conclude that my \(f\) is not differentiable (and hence not holomorphic) at any point \((x,y)\neq (0,0)\), and, by Proposition 5.4, it is differentiable (but still not holomorphic) at the origin.

Here is our first theoretical (quite natural) result that uses Cauchy–Riemann equations \(\eqref{eq5:1}\).

Proposition 5.8. Let \(E\) be a region, \(f\colon E\longrightarrow\C\) be holomorphic on \(E\), and \(f'(z)=0\) for all \(z\in E\). Then \(f\) must be constant.

Proof. Since \(f\) is assumed to be holomorphic, we have that Cauchy–Riemann equations hold at each point of \(E\) and moreover \[ u_x=u_y=v_x=v_y=0. \] This implies (by the mean value theorem for real functions) that both real functions \(u\) and \(v\) must be constant: \(u(x,y)=A,v(x,y)=B\). Therefore, \[ f(z)=A+\I B=C\in \C \] as expected. ■

Remark 5.9. Recall the definition of a region. The requirement that \(E\) is connected is essential here. It is easy to come up with a non-constant on disconnected set \(E\) function, whose derivative is however zero at every point.

5.2Harmonic functions

Very briefly, let me mention the connection of the holomorphic functions with harmonic ones. Note that equations \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) have a lot of symmetry. In particular, assume for a second that \(u,v\) have not only first partial derivatives, but also the second partial derivatives, which I assume to be continuous. Then I am allowed to differentiate both equations in \(\eqref{eq5:1}\). Taking the derivative with respect to \(x\) from the first equality I will find that \[ u_{xx}=v_{yx}, \] and differentiating the second equality with respect to \(y\) I get \[ u_{yy}=-v_{xy}. \] By my assumption (continuity of the second partial derivatives), \(v_{xy}=v_{yx}\) and hence \[ u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0. \] Similarly I can find that \[ v_{xx}+v_{yy}=0. \] This is one of the most important equations in mathematics (and especially in mathematical physics), which is called Laplace equation (to be precise, this is two dimensional Laplace equation, can you solve one dimensional one?). Any solution to the Laplace equation is called a harmonic function. Two harmonic functions that are connected by the Cauchy–Riemann equations \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) are called harmonic conjugate. We cannot prove it yet, but I would like to state that \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) is holomorphic on \(E\) if and only if both \(u\) and \(v\) are harmonic conjugates.

If \(E\) is simply connected (no holes!) and we know one of the harmonic conjugate, the second one can always be found as well. The easiest way to show this is by an example (and I also hope that you will have some useful recollections from Calc III and Intro to ODE).

Example 5.10. Let \[ u(x,y)=2 e^x\cos y \] be known. It is easy to check that \(u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0\), i.e., \(u\) is harmonic. What is its harmonic conjugate on \(\C\)?

I have \[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=2 e^x\cos y=\eqref{eq5:1}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\implies v(x,y)=\int 2 e^x\cos y\,\D y=2 e^x \sin y+\varphi(x). \] Moreover, \[ \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=2 e^{x}\sin y+\varphi'(x)=\eqref{eq5:1}= -\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=2 e^x\sin y\implies \varphi'(x)=0\implies \varphi(x)=C. \] If we fix one value of our \(f\), e.g., \(f(0)=2\), we can uniquely determine \[ f(z)=f(x+\I y)=2 e^{x}\cos y+2 \I e^{x}\sin y. \] Otherwise, the harmonic conjugate is determined up to a constant.

5.3Geometric meaning of \(f'(z)\) and conformal mappings

Recall that one possible geometric interpretation of \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) is a map from one plane, say \(z=x+\I y\), to another plane, \(w=u+\I v\). Here I will show the first very special property of holomorphic functions.

First, let us forget about \(f\) for a second and just assume that we have a pair of real valued functions, \(u\) and \(v\), that map the plane \((x,y)\) to the plane \((u,v)\). I start with a point \(M\) in plane \(z\) and a ray from this point that makes angle \(\theta\) with the real axis. In plane \((u,v)\) I will have point \(M^\ast=f(M)\) and a curve to which this ray is being mapped (I assume here that \(u,v\) do not map the plane \((x,y)\) to a point, or, in other words, \(u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y\) are not zero simultaneously). Let \(N\) be a point on my ray at distance \(\rho\) from \(M\) and \(N^\ast\) be its image under \(f\), \(N^\ast=f(N)\). Consider the ratio of the corresponding distances \[ \frac{M^\ast N^\ast}{MN}=\frac{1}{\rho}\sqrt{(u(x+\rho\cos \theta,y+\rho\sin\theta)-u(x,y))^2+(v(x+\rho\cos \theta,y+\rho\sin\theta)-v(x,y))^2}\,. \]

Now I take the limit \(\rho\to 0\) assuming that \(u,v\) are differentiable. In this case I am allowed to use l’Hôpital’s rule, which yields \[ \lambda=\lim_{\rho\to 0}\frac{M^\ast N^\ast}{MN}=\sqrt{(u_x \cos\theta+u_y\sin\theta)^2+(v_x \cos\theta+v_y\sin\theta)^2}\,. \]

Quantity \(\lambda\) is called linear magnification ratio at point \(M\) along the given ray \(MN\). Let us ask the following question: When does \(\lambda\) not depend on \(\theta\)? Rewriting \[ \lambda^2=A\cos^2\theta+2B\cos\theta\sin\theta+C\sin^2\theta, \] where \[ A=(u_x)^2+(v_x)^2,\quad B=u_xu_y+v_xv_y,\quad C=(u_y)^2+(v_y)^2, \] I obtain that \(\lambda\) does not depend on \(\theta\) if and only if \[ A=C,\quad B=0. \]

Exercise 2. Fill in the details in the statement above. I.e., prove that \(\lambda\) does not depend on \(\theta\) if and only if \begin{equation}\label{eq5:2} u_x^2+v_x^2=u_y^2+v_y^2,\quad u_xu_y+v_xv_y=0. \end{equation}

Now let me calculate the angle between \(MN\) and \(M^\ast N^\ast\). For \(MN\) I have \[ \mu=\tan \theta, \] for \(M^\ast N^\ast\) it is \[ \frac{v(x+\rho\cos \theta,y+\rho\sin\theta)-v(x,y)}{u(x+\rho\cos \theta,y+\rho\sin\theta)-u(x,y)}\,, \] which tends to \[ \tan \varphi=\nu=\frac{v_x\cos \theta+v_y\sin \theta}{u_x\cos \theta+u_y\sin \theta}=\frac{v_x+v_y\mu}{u_x+u_y\mu}\,, \] as \(\rho\to 0\).

Now (using a trigonometric formula for \(\tan(\varphi-\theta)\)) \[ \xi=\tan \zeta=\tan(\varphi-\theta)=\frac{\nu-\mu}{1+\nu\mu}=\frac{v_x+(v_y-u_x)\mu-u_y\mu^2}{u_x+(u_y+v_x)\mu+v_y\mu^2}\,. \] \(\zeta\) is called the rotation of the map at point \(M\) with respect to the given ray. Again, this rotation depends on both the coordinates \((x,y)\) of \(M\) and the chosen angle \(\theta\). Let us ask again the same question: When does the rotation at \(M\) not depend on \(\theta\)?

Looking at the last expression for \(\xi\) I conclude that rotation at \(M\) will not depend on \(\theta\) if and only if \begin{equation}\label{eq5:3} v'_x=\xi u'_x,\quad (v'_y-u_x')=\xi(u'_y+v'_x),\quad -u'_y=\xi v'_y, \end{equation} where \(\xi\) does not depend on \(\theta\).

Now to the main definition.

Definition 5.11. Map \((x,y)\mapsto \bigl(u(x,y),v(x,y)\bigr)\) is called conformal at \(M\in E\) if both magnification and rotation do not depend on the direction \(\theta\). It is called conformal on region \(E\) if it is conformal at every point of \(E\)

Why do we care at all? Recall from elementary geometry that we call figures similar if the corresponding angles are the same, and the corresponding sides are proportional to each other with the same coefficient of proportionality. Note the first corresponds to the invariance of rotation with respect to the given direction (indeed, if I now have two ray emanating out of the same point, and rotation does not depend on the chosen direction, they both will be rotated by the same angle) and the second one corresponds to the invariance of magnification with respect to given direction. In other words, conformal maps generalize the notion of similarity in a small neighborhood of the given point. This is sometimes quite important and historically was necessary when one tries to present a map of a sufficiently big part of the Earth (which is almost a sphere) on a plane. Very often some maps are required to be conformal.

Almost immediately, I have

Proposition 5.12. Let \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) be holomorphic on \(E\). Then \(f\) is conformal at every point \(z\) where \(f'(z)\neq 0\).

Proof. Using \(\eqref{eq5:1}\), I have that both \(\eqref{eq5:2}\) and \(\eqref{eq5:3}\) hold, which concludes the proof. ■

More interesting is the converse question. Namely, if one assumes that \(f=u+\I v\) is a conformal map, can we say anything about \(f\)? The answer is yes. Skipping technical details (not complicated but left as an exercise), I state

Proposition 5.13. Let \(\eqref{eq5:3}\) hold. Then it implies that \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) must hold.

Let \(\eqref{eq5:2}\) hold. Then it implies that either \(\eqref{eq5:1}\) holds or \[ u_x=-v_y,\quad u_y=v_x. \]

According to the last proposition, we are almost there. One thing to notice is that the second pair of conditions that are consequences of the invariance of magnification imply that in the complex world we deal with the maps of the form \(w=f(\bar z)\), where \(f\) is holomorphic (think this out!). Such maps keep the magnification ratio but in addition to the conformal map that is performed by \(f\) it adds also a reflection with respect to the \(x\)-axis (recall the geometric meaning of complex conjugation) and hence do not keep the rotation invariant (they change the orientation). They do, however, keep the angles between two curves the same, and for this reason often called anticonformal or conformal maps of the second kind.

Hence, to make it official,

Corollary 5.14. Let \(E\) be a domain, and \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) be given. Then \(f\) is holomorphic on \(E\) if and only if the corresponding map \((x,y)\mapsto (u(x,y),v(x,y))\) is conformal at all points \(E\) (except for those where all four partial derivatives \(u_x,v_x,u_y,v_y\) are zero.)

Exercise 3. Consider the function \(f(z)=z^2\). It is conformal anywhere in \(\C\setminus\{0\}\). Show directly that this function, considered as a map from one plane to another, is not conformal at \(0\).

Now we can state the geometric meaning of complex derivative. Assume that \(f\) is holomorphic on \(E\). Hence I have (we already know that it does not depend on the direction \(\theta\), hence I can take, e.g., \(\theta=0\), and I also use the fact that \(f'(z)=u_x+\I v_x\)) \begin{align*} \lambda&=\sqrt{(u'_x)^2+(v'_x)^2}=|f'(z)|,\\ \xi&=\frac{v_x}{u_x}\implies \zeta =\Arg f'(z), \end{align*} or in words, the modulus of the derivative geometrically is the linear magnification ratio at this point, and the argument of the derivative (assuming \(f'(z)\neq 0\)) is the rotation at the same point.

Remark 5.15. The last conclusion could be obtained much simpler, but in this case we would not get the full characterization of conformal maps.

Indeed, using the definition of the derivative, I have \[ \lim_{\Delta z\to 0}\left|\frac{\Delta w}{\Delta z}\right|=|w'|, \] which is exactly what we found since \(|\Delta w|\) is the length of the interval in the \(w\) plane, and \(|\Delta z|\) is the length of the interval in the \(z\) plane, and, by the definition of the derivative, the direction is not important.

Moreover, \[ \lim_{\Delta z\to 0}\Arg\frac{\Delta w}{\Delta z}=\lim_{\Delta z\to 0}(\Arg \Delta w-\Arg \Delta z)=\Arg w', \] which exactly means that \(\arg w'\) is the angle by which the interval in \(z\) plane is rotated to get the image interval in \(w\) plane.

Example 5.16. Jumping a little ahead let me take \[ f(z)=\frac 1z\,. \] Since, as expected, \[ \left(\frac 1z\right)'=-\frac {1}{z^2}\,, \] then \[ |f'(z)|=\frac{1}{r^2}\,,\quad \Arg f'(z)=\Arg(-1)-\Arg(z^2)=\pi -2 \Arg z, \] which indicates that figures close to the origin are magnified by the action of \(f\), far from the origin — reduced.

Optional material

*5.4More about differentiability

The situation with Propositions 5.3 and 5.4 is not very satisfactory from a mathematical standpoint, and it would be desirable to have a clearer connection between the complex differentiability of \(f\) and real differentiability of its two real components \(u\) and \(v\). In this optional section I will try to provide a few extra details. For this let me recall what it means for a function \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \R^2\) to be (real) differentiable (this generalizes immediately to any number of dimensions, or even to arbitrary Banach spaces if you know this word, it is called Fréchet differentiability). We say that \(f\) is (Fréchet) differentiable at the point \(z\in E\) if \[ f(z+h)=f(z)+Ah+\phi(h)h, \] as \(h\to 0\), where \(A\colon \R^2\longrightarrow \R^2\) is a (real) linear map and \(\phi(h)\to 0\) as \(h\to 0\). Recall that \(A\) is real linear if \(A(\alpha z+w)=\alpha Az+Aw\) for all real \(\alpha\). Of course we can consider \(\R^2=\C\) as a complex vector space, and \(A\) will be complex linear if \(A(\alpha z+w)=\alpha Az+Aw\) for all complex \(\alpha\). It is almost immediate to conclude that the only complex linear maps on \(\C\) are \(c z\) for \(c\in \C\). It implies that \(f\) is complex differentiable if and only if it is real differentiable and its real derivative \(A\) is complex linear. This sounds complicated, so let me simplify it further, but for this I will need to bring in a few extra facts.

From real analysis it is known that if \(f\) is real differentiable then its derivative is given by its Jacobi matrix \[ A=\begin{bmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \\ \end{bmatrix}. \] From one of the previous lectures we know that multiplication by complex number \(c=a+\I b\) can be represented by \(2\times 2\) matrix \[ \begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{bmatrix}, \] and hence we obtain

Proposition 5.17. \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) is holomorphic on \(E\) if and only if it is real differentiable on \(E\), and the real and imaginary parts of \(f\) satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations \[ u_x=v_y,\quad u_y=-v_x \] on \(E\).

Can one do better? And the answer is yes!

Since the details become more and more subtle, I would like just to state that it is true that

Proposition 5.18 (Looman–Menchoff theorem). A continuous \(f\colon E\longrightarrow \C\) is holomorphic on \(E\) if and only if it satisfies Cauchy–Riemann equations on \(E\).

If the reader wants to really dive into this particular discussion, for further study I recommend Gray, J. D., and S. A. Morris. “When is a function that satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations analytic?” The American Mathematical Monthly 85.4 (1978): 246–256.

*5.5A glimpse of Wirtinger’s calculus

Cauchy–Riemann equations can be written in a different way. Without going into many details, I would like just to mention it, since in some sense it looks much cleaner (although one should be careful while using the introduced below operators).

Let \(f\) be complex valued function considered as a function of two real variables \(x,y\). Then, as we learn in real analysis, its differential, \(\D f\), is given by \[ \D f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\D x+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\D y. \] In particular, I have \[ \D z=\D x+\I \D y,\quad \D \bar z=\D x-\I \D y. \] From the last equalities I can find \[ \D x=\frac 12(\D z+\D \bar z),\quad \D y=\frac{1}{2\I}(\D z-\D\bar z). \] Plugging these expression into the formula for \(\D f\) and rearranging, I find \[ \D f=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}-\I \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)\D z+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\I \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)\D\bar z. \] This prompts me to define two operators \(\partial f\) and \(\bar{\partial} f\) as follows (note, strictly speaking they are not partial derivatives!) \[ \partial f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}-\I \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right),\quad \bar\partial f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\I \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) \] so that I can write \[ \D f=\partial f\D z+\bar{\partial} f\D\bar z. \] These operators are called Wirtinger’s derivatives.

Now to the magic of the right notation. I claim that \(f\) is holomorphic if and only if \[\bar \partial f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}}=0.\] Moreover, if \(f\) is holomorphic then its Wirtinger derivative \(\partial f\) agrees with the usual one \[ \D f=\partial f\D z \] or \[ f'=\frac{\D f}{\D z}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}. \]

Exercise 4. Prove the last statements using the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

It can be proved (nothing other than calculus is required here, so I will leave it to you to practice your skills in partial differentiation) that Wirtinger’s derivatives are linear operators, satisfy the usual product and chain rules, and also have the conjugation property that \[ \overline{\partial f}=\bar \partial \bar f,\quad \overline{\bar{\partial} f}=\partial \bar f. \] Using these properties one can justify a natural calculation of the form \[ \partial (z^3+z^2\bar z)=3z^2+2z\bar z \] and similar ones.

So in some sense “\(f\) is holomorphic only if it has no \(\bar z\) in it, i.e., \(\bar\partial f=0\).” Do not take this statement literally though.

To conclude here is a cool application of Wirtinger’s derivatives.

Example 5.19. Show that the function \(g(z)=\log |f(z)|\) is harmonic on \(E\) if \(f\) is holomorphic on \(E\) and \(f(z)\neq 0\). Note that here \(\log\) is our usual real natural logarithm from school.

Recall that \(g\) is harmonic if it satisfies \(\Delta g=g_{xx}+g_{yy}=0\). Using the definition of Wirtinger’s derivatives one can find (fill in the details) that \[ \Delta g=4\partial \bar{\partial} g. \] So all we need is to show that \(\partial\bar{\partial}(\log f\bar f)=0\). Now to the computations: \[ \bar{\partial}\log f\bar f=\frac{1}{f\bar f}(\bar f\bar\partial f+f\bar{\partial}\bar f)=\mbox{($f$ is holomorphic!)}=\frac{\bar{\partial}\bar f}{\bar f}\,. \] Next, \[ \partial \frac{\bar{\partial}\bar f}{\bar f}=\frac{\bar f\partial\bar{\partial}\bar f-\partial\bar f\cdot\bar\partial \bar f}{\bar f^2}=\frac{1}{\bar f}\bar \partial(\partial \bar f)=0 \] and we are done (note that I used twice the fact that \(f\) is holomorphic in the last line).
