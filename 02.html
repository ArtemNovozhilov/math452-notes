2.1Definition

I will start finally with a precise definition, assuming that such mathematical object as vector space \(\R^2\) is well familiar to the students. Recall that any element of \(\R^2\) is a (column) vector \[ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \end{bmatrix}, \] where \(x,y\) are just two real numbers.

Definition 2.1. The set of complex numbers, usually denoted as \(\C\) (another standard notation is \(\mathbb C\), but I will stick to the former one), is, by definition, the vector space \(\R^2\), i.e., the set of pairs of real numbers, with the operations of addition and multiplication for any \(z_1,z_2\in \C\) defined \[ z_1+z_2=\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \\ \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} x_1+x_2 \\ y_1+y_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ z_1\cdot z_2=z_1z_2=\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \\ \end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} x_1x_2-y_1y_2 \\ x_1y_2+y_1x_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}. \]

The motivation to define the multiplication exactly as it is written above comes from “naive” manipulations with numbers containing square root of \(-1\) introduced to find the roots of a cubic polynomial, see the previous lecture.

Recall that the standard basis of \(\R^2\) is \(e_1=[1\,\,\,0]^\top,e_2=[0\,\,\, 1]^\top\) (where \(^\top\) denotes, as usual, the transposition), which literally means that any vector from \(\R^2\) (and hence from \(\C\)) can be represented in a unique way as a linear combination \[ z=\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \end{bmatrix}=x e_1+y e_2. \]

Now note that using the multiplication defined above for any \(z\) I have \(e_1\cdot z=z\cdot e_1=z\), so with a slight abuse of notation I will denote \(e_1\) as \(1\) emphasizing that it is my unit (probably a somewhat more precise notation would be \(1_{\C}\), but it also clutters the formulas more). Moreover, \[ e_2\cdot e_2=e_2^2=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}=-e_1=-1, \] therefore, if I introduce the notation \(\I=e_2\), then the last line reads (familiar, isn’t it?) \[ \I^2=-1, \] and hence the usual expression \[ z=x+\I y \] can (and should) be understood as a representation of \(z\in \C\) with respect to the standard basis \(\{1,\I\}\). No “imaginary” quantities anymore! Anyway, using symbol \(\I\) saves so much space and time (instead of writing vectors) and makes the computations so much easier that we will always use the notation \(x+\I y\) instead of \([x\,\,y]^\top\), but the student should remember that behind it there are always a couple of our very familiar vectors that form the standard basis of \(\R^2\), the only difference from \(\R^2\) is that now we are allowed to multiply our vectors among themselves.

So, what is so special about the introduced two arithmetic operations on the set of pairs of real numbers? Addition certainly looks natural, but why this somewhat strange rule for multiplication? Yes, in the first lecture we saw that such defined multiplication helps finding real roots of cubic equations by employing some “imaginary” computations with square root of \(-1\). Is it enough to justify the whole semester of studying complex numbers and (later) functions? To answer this question, let me first briefly discuss what makes the real numbers \(\R\) so special. Of course, we all know that there are two key operations on the set \(\R\): addition and multiplication. But these natural operations are not arbitrary, they satisfy a number of very useful properties that, after years in school, we take for granted, and yet these properties of addition and multiplication are exactly what makes the real numbers so indispensable. Let me start with addition. Addition of real numbers is commutative and associative, meaning that \[ a+b=b+a,\quad a+(b+c)=(a+b)+c \] for any \(a,b,c\in \R\). There is a special real number \(0\) that satisfies \(a+0=0+a=a\). For any real \(a\) there is additive inverse \(b\) for which \(a+b=b+a=0\), and we usually denote \(b=-a\) thus introducing the inverse operation to addition — subtraction. Now to the multiplication. Multiplication of real numbers is commutative and associative, meaning that \[ ab=ba,\quad a(bc)=(ab)c \] for any \(a,b,c\in \R\). There is a special number \(1\) that satisfies \(a1=1a=a\). For any nonzero \(a\in\R\) there is a multiplicative inverse \(b\) such that \(ab=ba=1\), and we usually denote \(b=a^{-1}=\frac{1}{a}\) thus introducing the inverse operation to multiplication — division.

Finally, these operations work perfectly fine together, i.e., the multiplication is distributive over addition: \[ a(b+c)=ab+ac. \] I hope that there is no question about the validity of the mentioned properties. Mathematically speaking, any set with two operations \(+\) and \(\cdot\), that satisfy the listed properties, is called a field. That is, above I have reminded you that the real numbers form a field. The cool part of Definition 2.1 is that the addition and multiplication of Definition 2.1 make the set of pairs of real numbers into a field as well! Specifically,

Theorem 2.2. Consider set \(\C\) with two operations \(+\) and \(\cdot\) defined in Definition 2.1. Then the addition is commutative and associative, there is a special element \(0\in \C\) such that \(z+0=0+z=z\) for any \(z\in\C\), for any \(z\in\C\) there is an additive inverse \(-z\) such that \(z+(-z)=0\). The multiplication is commutative and associative, there is a special element \(1\in \C\) such that \(1z=z1=z\) for any \(z\in \C\), any nonzero \(z\in\C\) has a multiplicative inverse \(z^{-1}\) such that \(zz^{-1}=1\). Finally, the multiplication is distributive over addition. In short, the set \(\C\) is a field.

I will not go over a proof of all the statements above, they are pretty immediate. The only confusing point could be about the existence of multiplicative inverse. But before showing how to find \(z^{-1}\) let me briefly discuss the relation between the sets \(\R\) and \(\C\).

Since the elements of \(\R\) are real numbers and elements of \(\C\) are pairs of real numbers, it is, strictly speaking, incorrect to say that \(\R\) is a subset of \(\C\). However, in many instances this is exactly what is being said. The point is that, if we consider (now a legitimate) subset of \(\C\) which consists of elements \([a\,\,0]^\top\) (the second coordinate is zero), it is clear that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the real numbers and elements of this set. Moreover, to add or multiply two elements from this set using the rules of Definition 2.1 is the same (check it) as to add or multiply two real numbers and just write an extra zero as a second coordinate. In other words, the set of such pairs \([a\,\,0]^\top\) is a subfield of \(\C\) and this set is isomorphic (you can interpret this word as “identical”) to the field \(\R\). Therefore, when talking about complex numbers, and suddenly saying that the result of some operation is real, it is usually meant that the result is of the form \([a\,\,0]^\top\), which is identified with real number \(a\).

Back to the multiplicative inverse. Here is a trick, which makes the division of two complex numbers immediate. For a complex \(z=x+\I y\in\C\) I define its complex conjugate \(\bar z=x-\I y\) and note that \[ z\bar z=(x+\I y)(x-\I y)=x^2+y^2 \] is a real number (which rigorously, again, should be understood as a vector in \(\R^2\) with the second coordinate zero, or even better as \((x^2+y^2)e_1\), but very soon we will stop even mentioning this).

Now, if I want to find \(z^{-1}\) I consider (again, as in the first lecture, I multiply and divide by the same nonzero quantity) \[ z^{-1}=\frac{1}{z}=\frac{\bar z}{z\bar z}=\frac{x-\I y}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}+\I\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,. \] Now you can directly check that for any nonzero \(z\in \C\), \(zz^{-1}=z^{-1}z=1\). The usual argument can be then used to prove the uniqueness of the inverse: \(zw=1\) implies \(z^{-1}zw=z^{-1}\), and hence \(w=z^{-1}\).

Similarly, if I want to divide complex number \(z_1=x_1+\I y_1\) by another complex number \(z_2=x_2+\I y_2\neq 0\) (that is, I need to compute \(z_1z_2^{-1}\)) I do the following: \[ \frac{z_1}{z_2}=\frac{z_1\bar z_2}{z_2\bar z_2}=\frac{1}{x_2^2+y_2^2}z_1\bar z_2=\frac{x_1x_2+y_1y_2}{x_2^2+y_2^2}+\I\frac{x_2y_1-x_1y_2}{x_2^2+y_2^2}=a+\I b\,, \] which is defined if and only if \(x_2^2+y_2^2\neq 0\), i.e., if and only if \(z_2\neq 0\) (i.e., if \(z_2\) is not a zero vector, the usual “one is not allowed to divide by zero” works here as well, as expected). I remark that no one remembers the formula for the division of two complex numbers, all you need to remember is to multiply and divide by the conjugate to the denominator.

As I mentioned above, all other properties of the field of complex numbers are immediate, and yet I ask you to work on the following exercises.

I remark that since \(\R\) is a field and \(\C\) is a field, all the algebraic formulas we got used to while manipulating expressions with real numbers stay the same if real numbers replaced with complex ones. To give one example, Newton’s binomial theorem is equally valid for any two complex numbers \(z\) and \(w\) (so if you have spent some time after reading the previous lecture to prove this theorem, now you do not need to prove anything, all the justification has been done) \[ (z+w)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n{n\choose k}z^{n-k}w^k, \] for any positive integer \(n\).

Before moving to the geometric interpretation of complex numbers let me introduce notation \(\Re z\) and \(\Im z\) for the real and imaginary parts of the complex number \(z=x+\I y\), which are defined as \(\Re z=x,\,\Im z=y\) respectively. Keeping in mind discussed above relation between \(\R\) and \(\C\) we say that complex number \(z\in\C\) is real, and write \(z\in\R\) if \(\Im z=0\), and complex number \(z\) is imaginary (or, better, purely imaginary) if \(\Re z=0\). Note that none of \(\Re z\) and \(\Im z\) involves the imaginary unit \(\I\).

2.2Basic geometry of complex numbers

In retrospect it is quite mysterious that the idea that the complex numbers are nothing other than points on the plane, which is so natural today, took over 250 years after solving the cubic equations to be expressed almost simultaneously by Caspar Wessel in 1799, Jean-Robert Argand in 1806, and finally by Carl Gauss in 1831. (Gauss is certainly the only one, who had a universal fame, two other presentations went mostly unnoticed, although the complex plane is still sometimes called Argand’s plane even in modern treatments. Also I would like to note that even before Gauss a lot of basic properties of complex numbers were discovered, in particular by Leonhard Euler, who pretty much knew all the properties of elementary functions of complex variable.)

Having at our disposal the fact that complex numbers are literally the vectors from \(\R^2\) (or sometimes it is more convenient to look at them as the points in the plane, which is completely equivalent since there is one-to-one correspondence between the vectors, i.e., the arrows emanating from the origin, and the points), we start with very basic terminology, illustrating it with geometric pictures.

For each complex number \(z=x+\I y\) we have a vector \([x\,\,\,y]^\top\) and/or a point with (rectangular or Cartesian) coordinates \((x,y)\). The coordinate \(x\) is called the real part of \(z\), and \(y\) is called the imaginary part of \(z\), the corresponding notations were introduced above. Note that geometrically conjugation is simply a reflection with respect to the \(x\)-axis, which is, for obvious reasons, called the real axis (the \(y\)-axis is called the imaginary axis for the same reasons). The whole plane \(\R^2\) is called the complex plane \(\C\) (for those who like absolute correctness of terms: Note that mathematically correct way would be to say that the real plane \(\R^2\) is the same as complex line \(\C=\C^1\), since the dimension of \(\C\) as a vector space over the field \(\C\) is obviously 1, but (almost) no one says this). The complex numbers on the real axis are identified with the usual real numbers, and the numbers on the imaginary axis are called purely imaginary.

Since \(z\in \C\) is geometrically a vector (see the figure), we can always calculate its length \(|z|\), which is given, by Pythagoras’ theorem, \[ |z|=|x+\I y|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,, \] and all the square roots of real numbers in these notes should be understood as unique positive square roots if not stated otherwise. The length of the vector \(z\) is naturally called its modulus, or absolute value. Continuing the analogy with real numbers, I remind that for \(x\in\R\), its absolute value \(|x|\) is the distance from the point \(x\) to the origin on the real line \(\R\). By looking at Fig. 1 we see that for complex number \(z=x+\I y\in\C\), its modulus, or length, or absolute value, \(|z|\) is the distance from the point \((x,y)\) to the origin. Moreover, if \(z\in \R\) is real, I get, in line with all the previous discussion, \(|z|=\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), so they agree on the set of real numbers.

I hope that all the students remember that for the usual absolute value \(|x|\) of a real number \(x\) it is true that \(|x+y|\leq |x|+|y|\) (can you prove this inequality?). It turns out the same is true for any complex \(z,w\in\C\): \begin{equation}\label{eq2:1} |z+w|\leq |z|+|w|. \end{equation} It is not difficult to prove it algebraically, but much nicer just to see it geometrically. For this note that addition of complex numbers is actually our usual addition of vectors (by a triangle or parallelogram law, see Fig. 2), and hence inequality \(\eqref{eq2:1}\) compares the length of one side of a triangle with the sum of the lengths of two other sides, and therefore becomes obvious (can you see when the equality in this formula holds?). Similarly, by looking at the geometric interpretation of complex subtraction (which follows from the fact that \(z-w=z+(-w)\) and geometrically vector \(-w\) is the reflection of vector \(w\) with respect to the origin), see Fig. 2, right panel, I conclude that \begin{equation}\label{eq2:2} \bigl||z|-|w|\bigr|\leq|z-w|. \end{equation}

While both \(\eqref{eq2:1}\) and \(\eqref{eq2:2}\) are certainly correct for any complex numbers \(z,w\in\C\), a picture is not a proof. So in the next exercise I ask you to prove, among other things, both \(\eqref{eq2:1}\) and \(\eqref{eq2:2}\) algebraically. To do this in a simplest possible way, it is important to remember that dealing with modulus directly is usually not very productive. Much better is to notice that, according to our computations, we have the relation \[ |z|^2=x^2+y^2=z\bar z=(x+\I y)(x-\I y). \] Using the conjugation is much nicer to work with, since, e.g., \(\overline{z+w}=\bar z+\bar w\) but \(|z+w|\neq |z|+|w|\) in general.

Exercise 6. Prove algebraically (i.e., using just the allowed manipulations with formulas) the following: \begin{align*} |z|&=|\bar z|,\\ |zw|&=|z||w|,\\ |\Re z|&\leq |z|,\\ |\Im z|&\leq |z|,\\ |z|&\leq |\Re z|+|\Im z|,\\ |z+w|&\leq |z|+|w|,\\ |z_1+z_2+\ldots+z_n|&\leq |z_1|+|z_2|+\ldots+|z_n|,\\ \bigl||z|-|w|\bigr|&\leq|z-w|. \end{align*}

There is no straightforward geometric interpretation of multiplication of complex numbers in (rectangular) coordinates \((x,y)\) (and yet see Remark 2.4), but remember that on the plane \(\R^2\) we always can pass to polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\).

Recall that the polar coordinates of point \(A\) are given by the distance \(r\) of this point from the origin and by the angle \(\theta\) of the vector \(OA\) with the polar axis (if \(A\) coincides with the origin the angle is undetermined). Here is one subtle thing: polar coordinates are not defined uniquely since, e.g., angles \(\theta\) and \(\theta+2\pi k,\,k\in\Z\) are indistinguishable. I have, using the basic trigonometry, that for the complex number \(z=x+\I y\), its polar distance is \[ r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}, \] and the corresponding angle, which is called an argument of the complex number \(z\), satisfies \[ \tan \theta=\frac{y}{x}\,. \]

I will denote argument by \(\Arg z\), where the capital letter emphasizes that it is a set of the form \(\{\varphi+2\pi k,\,k\in\Z\}\), where \(\varphi\) is any of the infinitely many arguments, and not a unique number. In other words \(|z|\) and \(\Arg z\) are the polar coordinates \(r,\theta\) of the point with rectangular coordinates \((x,y)\). There is only one argument that satisfies the condition \(-\pi<\theta\leq \pi\), and I will call this (unique) argument of \(z\) as the principal argument, \(\theta=\arg z\) (I note that in some textbooks the notation \(\Arg\) and \(\arg\) is used in the opposite way, in my notes the first capital letter will always mean that the result is multivalued, which is very common in complex analysis, get used to it).

Now using \(\tan \theta=y/x\) I would like to determine \(\arg z\). Since \(\arctan \) maps any real number to the interval \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\) I must have (make a figure if you are confused here) \[ \arg z=\begin{cases} \arctan\frac{y}{x}\,,&x>0,\\ \arctan\frac{y}{x}+\pi,&x<0,y\geq 0,\\ \arctan\frac{y}{x}-\pi,&x<0,y<0,\\ \frac{\pi}{2}\,,&x=0,y>0,\\ -\frac{\pi}{2}\,,&x=0,y<0,\\ \mbox{indeterminate},&x=y=0. \end{cases} \]

We of course have (see Fig. 1) \(\arg z=-\arg \bar z\) (is it true for any \(z\)?), remember that the positive angles correspond to the counterclockwise direction, and the negative angles correspond to the clockwise direction.

To go back from polar coordinates to rectangular, one uses \[ x=r\cos \theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta, \] and hence \[ z=x+\I y=r(\cos\theta+\I\sin\theta)=|z|(\cos \Arg z+\I\sin\Arg z), \] which gives us a relation between the rectangular and polar forms of complex number \(z\).

Finally I am ready to see the geometric meaning of complex multiplication. Let \(z_1,z_2\) be two complex numbers \[ z_1=r_1(\cos \theta_1+\I\sin\theta_1),\quad z_2=r_2(\cos \theta_2+\I\sin\theta_2). \] I have (using some basic school trigonometry for sine and cosine) \begin{align*} z_1z_2&=r_1r_2\bigl((\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2)+\I(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\sin\theta_2\cos\theta_1)\bigr)\\ &=r_1r_2(\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)+\I \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)), \end{align*} in other words, \[ |z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|,\quad \Arg z_1z_2=\Arg z_1+\Arg z_2, \] where the last equality means that the set of all possible values \(\Arg z_1z_2\) is obtained by forming all possible sums from the sets \(\Arg z_1\) and \(\Arg z_2\). Once again in words: to multiply two complex numbers means geometrically to obtain the vector with the modulus which is the product of two moduli, and with the argument which is the sum of the corresponding arguments (make a figure for, say, \(z=1\) and \(w=\I\)).

Since \[z^{-1}=\frac{\bar z}{|z|^2},\quad \frac{w}{z}=wz^{-1},\] and the modulus of \(|z^{-1}|=1/|z|\) and argument \(\Arg z^{-1}=-\Arg z\), where the last equality understood modulo \(2\pi\), then \[ \left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}\,,\quad \Arg\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\Arg z_1-\Arg z_2\,. \]

Finally two remarks to close this section.

Remark 2.3. Note that I derived a few inequalities in this section, but they all are between the real numbers, which we know how to compare. Mathematically speaking, the set \(\R\) is not just a field, it is an ordered field, which literally means that if \(\R\ni x\neq 0\) then exactly one of \(x>0\) or \(-x>0\) holds, and if \(\R\ni x,y>0\) then both \(x+y>0\) and \(xy>0\) hold. In other words, on the set \(\R\) there exists order relation \(>\), which is compatible with the field operations \(+\) and \(\cdot\). It turns out that on \(\C\) it is not possible, i.e., there exists no order relation on \(\C\) compatible with the field operations as defined in Definition 2.1. I invite you to prove it (by contradiction, start, e.g., with the assumption that \(\I>0\)). So, when you see in any mathematical text an expression of the form \(a>0\) then it immediately implies that \(a\in \R\) (or \(a\in\Q\), or \(a\in\Z\), etc), for complex number \(a\) with nonzero imaginary part it does not make any sense.

Remark 2.4. (This remark is optional, but useful to connect the complex numbers with the geometry of the plane.) Let me look at the multiplication of complex numbers from a different perspective. Consider the product \(zw\), which takes complex number \(w\) and returns \(zw\), in short, \(w\mapsto zw\). It is clearly a linear map from \(\C\) to \(\C\), since \(z(\alpha w+v)=\alpha(zw)+zv\) for any complex \(\alpha, w, v\) by the properties of complex multiplication. Recall that for us \(\C\) is (almost) \(\R^2\), and from the course of linear algebra we know that any linear operator on \(\R^2\) can be represented by a \(2\times 2\) matrix in the standard basis. To determine this matrix all we need is to see how it acts on the basis vectors \(1\) and \(\I\). So, if \(z=a+\I b\), then \(z\cdot 1=a+\I b\) and \(z\cdot \I=-b+\I a\), in other words, our matrix takes the form \[ \begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{bmatrix}. \] Therefore, we can also say that the complex numbers are represented not only by vectors from \(\R^2\), but also by \(2\times 2\) matrices of the special form such that \(a_{11}=a_{22}\) (which is the real part) and \(a_{21}=-a_{12}\) (which is the imaginary part). Now the multiplication of complex numbers is nothing else other than the usual multiplication of two matrices, e.g., \[ zw=(a+\I b)(c+\I d)=\begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c & -d \\ d & c \\ \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} ac-bd & -(ad+bc) \\ ad+bc & ac-bd \\ \end{bmatrix}=(ac-bd)+\I (ad+bc). \] (And the addition is of course the usual addition.)

Note that if \(a^2+b^2\neq 0\) then \[ z=\begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{bmatrix}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\begin{bmatrix} \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}} & \frac{-b}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}} \\ \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}} & \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}} \\ \end{bmatrix}=|z|\begin{bmatrix} \cos\Arg z & -\sin \Arg z \\ \sin \Arg z & \cos \Arg z \\ \end{bmatrix}, \] and I hope that the student recognizes the rotation matrix on the right, which exactly corresponds to adding an argument of \(z\) to the argument of the complex number, which is being multiplied by \(z\).

2.3Powers and roots

For any natural \(n\) I have \[ z^n=z\cdot\ldots\cdot z. \] Using the formula for complex multiplication in polar form derived above yields \[ z^n=\bigl(r(\cos\theta+\I\sin\theta)\bigr)^n=r^n(\cos n\theta+\I\sin n\theta). \] If \(r=1\) then I obtain de Moivre’s theorem (or de Moivre’s formula) \[ (\cos\theta+\I\sin\theta)^n=\cos n\theta+\I\sin n\theta. \] If I define \(z^0=1\) and \(z^{-n}=\frac{1}{z^n}\) then de Moivre’s theorem becomes true for any integer \(n\in\Z\) (do you see why?). I will use this formula to solve the equation \[ z^n=1, \] in other words I want to determine all possible \(n\)-th roots \(1^{\frac{1}{n}}\) of unity \(1\).

Since \(|1|=1\) and \(\Arg 1=2\pi k\) for \(k\in \Z\), I have, assuming that \(z=r(\cos \theta+\I\sin\theta)\), \[ r^n(\cos n\theta+\I\sin n\theta)=\cos 2\pi k+\I\sin 2\pi k, \] which implies (I use here the fact that if two complex numbers are equal then their polar coordinates are equal, remembering that \(\Arg\) is not defined uniquely) that \(r^n=1\implies r=1\), and \[ \theta=\frac{2\pi k}{n}\,,\quad k\in \Z. \] How many really different \(\theta\) did I get? Note that for \(k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\) all \(\theta\) will be between 0 and \(2\pi\), but for \(k=n\) I get \(\theta=2\pi\), which corresponds to the same polar angle as \(\theta=0\), for \(k=n+1\) I get \(\theta=\frac{2\pi}{n}+2\pi\), which corresponds to \(k=1\), etc. Therefore I obtained

Proposition 2.5. Equation \(z^n=1\) has always \(n\) distinct roots \[ \hat z_k=1^{\frac{1}{n}}=\cos \frac{2\pi k}{n}+\I \sin \frac{2\pi k}{n}\,,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1, \] which are called the \(n\)-th roots of unity.

In exactly the same way I can solve the equation \[ z^n=w \] for a given nonzero complex \(w=\rho(\cos \varphi+\I\sin\varphi)\).

Fill in the missing details to convince yourself that in this case \[ z_k=w^{\frac{1}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{\rho}\left(\cos\frac{\varphi+2\pi k}{n}+\I \sin\frac{\varphi+2\pi k}{n}\right),\quad k=0,\ldots,n-1, \] where \(\varphi\) is the principal (it is not necessary, but I take \(\varphi=\arg w\) to be specific) argument of \(w\). Note that \(\sqrt[n]{\rho}\) is the unique positive \(n\)-th root of \(\rho>0\). Using the formula for the multiplication of complex numbers implies that \[ z_k=w^{\frac{1}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{\rho}\left(\cos\frac{\varphi}{n}+\I \sin \frac{\varphi}{n}\right)\hat z_k,\quad k=0,\ldots,n-1. \]

Exercise 7. \(\Arg z\) is a multivalued function. It would be nice to work with the single valued \(\arg z\), but in this case we also miss a lot. In particular, show that in general the equalities \[ \arg zw=\arg z+\arg w,\quad \arg \frac{z}{w}=\arg z-\arg w \] are incorrect (I am asking you to come up with examples that disprove these formulas).

Exercise 8. Give a plausible intuitive argument that the sum of all the \(n\)-th roots of unity is always zero (\(n\neq 1\)). What geometric fact does it express? (To reach a correct answer, you can try a few cases, e.g., solve \(z^3=1\), \(z^4=1\), \(z^8=1\).)

Actually, using the third roots of unity the formula that I derived can be modified to give all three (in general complex) roots (we will prove the fact that polynomial of degree \(n\) has exactly \(n\) complex roots after developing necessary machinery). 2.4Examples with solutions

Let us see how the material I discussed so far in this lecture can be used to solve concrete examples.

Example 2.6. What is the polar form of \(z=-1-\I\sqrt{3}\)? I have \[ |z|=\sqrt{1^2+(\sqrt{3})^2}=2,\quad \tan \theta=\tan\arg z=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{-1}=\sqrt{3}\implies \arg z=-\frac{2\pi}{3}\,, \] hence \[ -1-\I\sqrt{3}=2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)+\I\sin\left(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)\right). \]

Example 2.7. What is the modulus and argument of \[ z=-\sin\frac{\pi}{8}-\I\cos\frac{\pi}{8}? \] The modulus is easy since \[ |z|=\sqrt{\sin^2\frac{\pi}{8}+\cos^2\frac{\pi}{8}}=1. \] For the argument we have \[ \arg z=-\pi+\arctan \frac{\cos \frac \pi 8}{\sin \frac \pi 8}=-\pi+\arctan \cot \frac \pi 8=-\pi +\arctan \tan\left(\frac \pi 2-\frac \pi 8\right)=-\frac{5\pi}{8}, \] hence \[ \Arg z=-\frac{5\pi}{8}+2\pi k,\quad k\in\Z. \]

Example 2.8. Compute \((-1+\I\sqrt{3})^{60}\).

First \[ -1+\I\sqrt{3}=2\left(\cos \frac{2\pi}{3}+\I\sin \frac{2\pi}{3}\right). \] Hence by de Moivre’s formula \[ (-1+\I\sqrt{3})^{60}=2^{60}\left(\cos \frac{2\pi}{3}+\I\sin \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)^{60}=2^{60}\left(\cos 60\frac{2\pi}{3}+\I\sin 60\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)=2^{60}. \]

Example 2.9. Find a formula for \(\sin 3\theta\).

By de Moivre’s formula \[ (\cos \theta+\I\sin \theta)^3=\cos 3\theta+\I\sin 3\theta. \] Raising to the third power the left hand side (i.e., using Newton’s binomial theorem) I get \[ (\cos \theta+\I\sin \theta)^3=\cos^3\theta+3\I\cos^2\theta\sin\theta-3\cos\theta\sin^2 \theta-\I\sin^3\theta \] and recalling that two complex numbers are equal if and only if their real and imaginary parts are equal simultaneously, I conclude \[ \sin 3\theta=3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta-\sin^3\theta=3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta. \]

Example 2.10. Solve \(z^4=1-\I\). I have \[ 1-\I=\sqrt{2}\left(\cos\left(-\frac \pi 4\right)+\I\sin\left(-\frac \pi 4\right)\right), \] hence by the formula I have I get \[ z_k=\sqrt[8]{2}\left(\cos\frac{-\pi/4+2\pi k}{4}+\I\sin\frac{-\pi/4+2\pi k}{4}\right),\quad k=0,1,2,3. \]

Example 2.11. What curve is defined by the equation \[ |z+c|+|z-c|=2a,\quad c\in\R,\quad a\in\R,\quad a>c\geq 0? \] I can answer this question by writing \(z=x+\I y\), etc. but a better way is to notice that \(|z+c|\) is the distance from \(z\) to \(-c\) and \(|z-c|\) is the distance from \(z\) to \(c\). According to the equation the sum of these distances is constant . Therefore, this curve is an ellipse, which has the equation \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1,\quad b^2=a^2-c^2. \]

Example 2.12. What curve is defined by \[ \Re \frac{1}{z}=\frac 14? \] Here probably the algebraic approach is the simplest one. I have \[ \Re \frac{1}{z}=\frac{\frac{1}{\bar z}+\frac{1}{z}}{2}=\frac{z+\bar z}{2|z|^2}=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,, \] hence \[ x^2+y^2-4x=0, \] which is the circle \((x-2)^2+y^2=4\). To be careful, I need to remove the point \((0,0)\) from the answer, since division by 0 is not allowed.

Example 2.13. Write in complex form the equation of the straight line \[ Ax+By+C=0. \] Note that we can always use \(x=(\bar z+z)/2,y=\I(\bar z-z)/2\), hence \[ (A+\I B)\bar z+(A-\I B)z+2C=0, \] or, using \(a=A+\I B\), \[ a\bar z+\bar a z+2C=0. \]

Exercise 10. Let \(z\in\C\) and \(t\in \R\). How does the position of the point \(tz\) change if \(t\) is changing \((a)\) from 1 to \(+\infty\); \((b)\) from 1 to 0; \((c)\) from 0 to \(-\infty\)?

How does the position of the point \(z(\cos t+\I\sin t)\) change if \(t\) changes from \(0\) to \(2\pi\)? from \(2\pi\) to \(4\pi\)?

Exercise 11. Let \(z,w\in \C\). What is the position of the point \((z+w)/2\)? How does the position of the point \((1-t)z+tw\) change when \(t\) changes from 0 to 1?

Exercise 12. Let \(z_1,z_2,z_3\) be given three non-collinear points; \(t_1,t_2,t_3\) be given three positive numbers such that \(t_1+t_2+t_3=1\). Show that points \(\xi=t_1z_1+t_2z_2+t_3z_3\) are inside the triangle \(z_1z_2z_3\).

*2.5Quaternions and Octonions

At this point a curious student would necessarily have a few questions. For instance, is it possible to come up with a different way to multiply pairs of real numbers so that the obtained structure would be again a field, but different from the field of complex numbers? Or, is it possible to define multiplication (addition is “obvious”) of triples of real numbers? Or, in more generality, is it possible, starting with \(\R^n\) for arbitrary \(n\), to come up with a reasonable definition of multiplication of vectors?

Without going into subtle and complicated algebraic technicalities, I would like to state that, in some sense, there are only 3 dimensions, for which it is possible to define “reasonable” multiplication (you can find the exact statement if you google Frobenius theorem about real division algebras). Specifically, these are the cases \(n=1\), when we get the field of real numbers; \(n=2\), when we get the field of complex numbers, and the case \(n=4\), when one gets the number system, which was initially described by Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton in 1843 (he spent countless hours trying to come up with a rule to multiply triples of real numbers but failed, and for a good reason), and which is called quaternions and denoted \(\mathbf{H}\). If you followed the discussion of the rigorous definition of complex numbers, it should be clear that all one needs to come up with to define the multiplication of vectors from \(\R^4\) is the rules how to multiply the basis vectors. Everything else follows.

Let me denote \(\{1,\I,\J,\K\}\) the unit standard vectors of \(\R^4\). Then the rules of multiplication are given by \[ \I\J\K=\I^2=\J^2=\K^2=-1 \] and \[ \I\J=-\J\I=\K,\quad \J\K=-\K\J=\I,\quad \I\K=-\K\I=-\J, \] note that the multiplication of quaternions is not commutative (this is the price we are paying to keep all the other properties of the arithmetic operations of a field).

Quaternions did not find such wide applicability like the complex numbers, but they are often used in computer graphics to represent 3D rotations (note that, opposite to the planar case, rotations in three dimensional world are not commutative). You can find much more on the internet.

If we are willing to abandon not only commutativity of multiplication, but also associativity, one can construct the so-called octonions \(\mathbf{O}\), which, hopefully not surprisingly, have dimension \(8\). Some people claim that octonions may be useful for theoretical physics (general relativity and supersymmetry) but I know way too little about this to make any comments. Anyway, if you’d like to read about possible extensions of our familiar real numbers, you now know the terms to type into your favorite search engine.
