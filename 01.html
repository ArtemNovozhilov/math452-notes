How it all began with the complex numbers

Our goal in this course is to study the basics of Complex Analysis, which is, informally speaking, is a mathematical discipline that studies the properties of analytic (or holomorphic, do not worry, we’ll get to a precise definition in due time) functions of complex argument \(z\in\C\), where, as usual, notation \(z\in\C\) means that variable \(z\) belongs to the set \(\C\) of all complex numbers (another standard notation for the same set is \(\mathbb C\), but I will stick in these notes to the former one). But before embarking on our main goal, we need to carefully understand what a complex number is. Yes, of course, the usual answer by anyone who already saw the complex numbers, is as follows: a complex number \(z\) is an expression of the form \(a+\I b\), where \(a,b\in \R\) (recall that \(\R\) is the set of real numbers), and \(\I\) is the imaginary unit that has the characteristic property \(\I^2=-1\). But then I will ask: Still, what is “\(\I\)”? Can we have some other (physical, geometric) meaning of this creature other than this very strange, unmotivated, and even bizarre property of being negative one if squared? There is a very simple answer to this question (and, moreover, this answer is by no means unique!), but before providing it, I will give a short account of why something like \(\I^2=-1\) turned out to be, on one hand, necessary, and, on the other hand, somewhat mysterious or imaginary.

1.1A few words about several Italian mathematicians

Complex numbers how we know them were introduced to solve cubic equations of the form \[ x^3+ax^2+bx+c=0, \] where \(a,b,c\) are real numbers.

For thousands of years people knew how to solve the quadratic equation \[ x^2+px+q=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=\frac{-p\pm\sqrt{p^2-4q}}{2}\,, \] which may have either two (real) roots, or one real root, or no real roots at all depending on the sign of the expression \(\Delta=p^2-4q\), which is called the discriminant of quadratic equation. Contrary to what some textbooks say, there was no issue with these three distinct possibilities, and especially with the fact that sometimes the quadratic equation has no real roots at all, since there is a transparent geometric interpretation for this algebraic problem. Namely, let me rewrite my quadratic equation as \[ x^2=-px-q, \] and hence the roots of my equation geometrically are intersections of the quadratic parabola \(y=x^2\) and the straight line \(y=-px-q\). Clearly, no matter how I pick \(p,q\) and plot these two curves, it is only possible to have either two points of intersection, or one point, or no intersection at all (see Fig. 1).

It took much longer to figure our how to attack a general cubic equation. It seems that the first person, who actually was able to solve the so-called depressed cubic equation \[ x^3+px+q=0 \] with only positive \(p\) and \(q\) was Italian mathematician Scipione del Ferro (1465–1526). He did not accept negative numbers and therefore did not think that he discovered something very general (e.g., he would consider the problem \(x^3=px+q\) to be a completely different one). He kept his discovery as a secret, since there was a tradition at the time to have mathematical competitions with monetary prizes and hence this knowledge would allow him to make some cash. Only when he was dying he shared his secret with one of his students, Antonio Maria Fior, who decided to capitalize on this knowledge. In particular, he found out about Niccolo Fontana (1500–1577), who actually was a very bright mathematician, and who claimed that he figured the way to solve the cubic equations of the form \(x^3+px^2=q\). Fior belived that Fontana, who is nowadays more known under the nickname Tartaglia, was bluffing and challenged him. Tartaglia knew that Fior can solve depressed cubic equations and was able to discover the formula for the roots before the contest. So he was able to solve Fior’s problems, whereas his own problems were too complicated to Fior. Hence Tartaglia won the contest.

Here in our story comes Girolamo Cardano (1501–1576), yet another Italian mathematician, who convinced Tartaglia to share his secret solution under the oath of not publishing it (that happened around 1539). Cardano kept his secret till 1545 when he found out that already del Ferro had the same method. He published these results about the cubic equation in his Ars Magna (“The Great Art”) giving credit to both del Ferro and Tartaglia (who, by the way, was furious and challenged Cardano for contest, which Cardano refused). Ludovico Ferrari (1522–1565) was a student of Cardano, who accepted the challenge and won the contest (Tartaglia lost a lot of money and died in poverty; which is even more ironic, in the history of mathematics the formula that solves the cubic equation is nowadays known as Cardano’s formula despite the fact that the two people who must get the credit for it are del Ferro and Tartaglia). To jump ahead, in Cardano’s formula under certain circumstances, one needs to use square roots of negative numbers. Cardano himself did not include any such explicit calculations in his book, and arguably the first person with an explicit example was Rafael Bombelli (1526–1572), who wrote a book on algebra that was published in 1572, and in which for the first time the rules how to work with complex numbers were formulated.

1.2Cardano’s formula

To warm up, let us first recall how one can derive the formula for the roots of quadratic equation \[ ax^2+bx+c=0, \] where \(a,b,c\in\R\) and \(a\neq 0\). Since \(a\neq 0\) I can divide by \(a\) and complete the square, using the well known fact that for any real \(A,B\), \(A^2+2AB+B^2=(A+B)^2\). So, I have a chain of equivalent equalities (note that I am doing two operations that can always be performed without changing the value of an expression: I add and subtract the same number, and I multiply and divide by the same nonzero number, it is surprising how often these elementary tricks lead to substantial simplifications): \begin{align*} ax^2+bx+c=0&\Longleftrightarrow\\ x^2+2\frac{b}{2a}x+\frac{c}{a}=0&\Longleftrightarrow\\ x^2+2\frac{b}{2a}x+\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2-\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2+\frac{c}{a}=0&\Longleftrightarrow\\ \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2-\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}=0&\Longleftrightarrow\\ \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}&\Longleftrightarrow\\ x+\frac{b}{2a}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}&\Longleftrightarrow\\ x=\frac{-b\pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\,, \end{align*} and we recovered the familiar formula for the roots of the quadratic equation.

Following the example of the quadratic equation one can try to eliminate the quadratic term in a cubic equation by completing the cube, using the formula \(A^3+3A^2B+3AB^2+B^3=(A+B)^3\).

Let me start with a general cubic equation of the form \[ y^3+ay^2+by+c=0, \] where \(a,b,c\) are given (real) numbers, and, for notational simplicity, I already assumed that the leading coefficient at \(y^3\) is equal to 1. If not, I can always divide by this coefficient as my first step. I have \begin{align*} y^3+ay^2+by+c&=\\ y^3+3y^2\frac{a}{3}+3\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)^2y+\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)^3-3\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)^2y-\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)^3+by+c&=\\ \left(y+\frac{a}{3}\right)^3+y\left(b-\frac{a^2}{3}\right)+c-\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)^3&=0, \end{align*} which, after the substitution \(x=y+\frac{a}{3}\), becomes \begin{align*} x^3+\left(x-\frac{a}{3}\right)\left(b-\frac{a^2}{3}\right)+c-\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)^3&=\\ x^3+x\left(b-\frac{a^2}{3}\right)+\frac{2a^3}{3^3}-\frac{ab}{3}+c&=\\ x^3+3p x+2 q&=0, \end{align*} where I introduced new parameters \[ 3p=b-\frac{a^2}{3}\,,\quad 2q=\frac{2a^3}{3^3}-\frac{ab}{3}+c\,, \] the coefficients 3 and 2 are chosen to simplify the subsequent formulas a little bit.

Therefore, my first conclusion is that any general cubic equation can be reduced to the depressed form \begin{equation}\label{eq1:1} x^3+3px+2q=0. \end{equation}

The next step is the one, which is absolutely non obvious. To motivate it, I note that if I am able somehow to get rid of \(3px\) term in \(\eqref{eq1:1}\), then the problem would be solved. Sometimes it is nice to remember that raising to some power an expression of the form \(u-\frac{k}{u}\) cancels a lot of terms, so I am making an educated guess to try another substitution \[ x=u-\frac{k}{u}\,, \] where the constant \(k\) will be determined later (an educated guess in mathematics is very often called an ansatz).

I have, on one hand, \[ x^3=\left(u-\frac{k}{u}\right)^3=u^3-3u^2\frac{k}{u}+3u\frac{k^2}{u^2}-\frac{k^3}{u^3}=u^3-3uk+3\frac{k^2}{u}-\frac{k^3}{u^3}\,, \] and on the other hand \[ 3px=3p\left(u-\frac{k}{u}\right)=3pu-3\frac{pk}{u}\,. \] Comparing the last two expressions, I notice that choosing \(k=p\) in my substitution leads in the original equation to \[ u^3-\frac{p^3}{u^3}+2q=0, \] which is a quadratic equation with respect to \(u^3\): \[ u^6+2q u^3-p^3=0, \] which gives two solutions \[ u^3=-q\pm\sqrt{q^2+p^3}\,. \] Returning to variable \(x\), I get \[ x=\sqrt[3]{-q\pm \sqrt{q^2+p^3}}-\frac{p}{\sqrt[3]{-q\pm \sqrt{q^2+p^3}}}\,. \] There remains the question which sign to choose in my formulas, but I claim that it does not matter, the result will be the same for both “\(+\)” and “\(-\)”. Indeed, let me choose, e.g., “\(+\)”. Then, since \[ (-q+\sqrt{q^2+p^3})(-q-\sqrt{q^2+p^3})=q^2-q^2-p^3=-p^3, \] multiplying both numerator and denominator of the second term in my formula by its conjugate (at this point for us the conjugate of the expression \(A+B\) is \(A-B\), and I use \((A+B)(A-B)=A^2-B^2\)) and simplifying, I end up with \begin{equation}\label{eq1:2} x=\sqrt[3]{-q+ \sqrt{q^2+p^3}}+\sqrt[3]{-q- \sqrt{q^2+p^3}}, \end{equation} which is Cardano’s formula for the depressed cubic equation \(\eqref{eq1:1}\) (note that depending on the form of the depressed cubic, it is possible to have different forms of Cardano’s formula). I invite the reader to check that choosing sign “\(-\)” in the above formula leads to exactly the same expression.

To get some practice with Cardano’s formula, let’s solve \[ x^3-6x-6=0. \] I have that \(p=-2,q=-3\), which leads to \[ x=\sqrt[3]{3+\sqrt{3^2-2^3}}+\sqrt[3]{3-\sqrt{3^2-2^3}}=\sqrt[3]{4}+\sqrt[3]{2}, \] which can be directly checked to be a legitimate root of our problem. Is it possible to have other roots? Yes, of course, but if we already know one root, we can always use the long division algorithm to reduce our problem to a quadratic equation, whose solution we know (make sure that you clearly understand the meaning of the previous phrase).

So, the problem solved? Not so fast.

Look at \[ x^3-15 x-4=0, \] which yields, using Cardano’s formula with \(p=-5,q=-2\), \[ x=\sqrt[3]{2+11\sqrt{-1}}+\sqrt[3]{2-11\sqrt{-1}}\,. \] I am tempted to say that since I need to extract square roots from negative numbers it indicates that my problem has no solution, as I explained when I presented the formula for the quadratic equation. But geometrically the situation now is very different! According to \(\eqref{eq1:1}\), my roots are the intersections of the cubic parabola \(y=x^3\) and a straight line \(y=-3px-2q\). Clearly, no matter what \(p\) and \(q\) are, there must be at least one point of intersection (see Fig. 2). In other words: Any cubic equation has at least one (real) root.

For our specific example a few seconds of thought (or see Section 1.3 for an algorithmic approach) yields that the root we are looking for is \(x=4\). And it was actually Bombelli’s ingenious idea, after looking at Cardano’s formula, to assume that \[ 4=x=\sqrt[3]{2+11\sqrt{-1}}+\sqrt[3]{2-11\sqrt{-1}}=2+n\sqrt{-1}+2-n\sqrt{-1}=4, \] where I need only to determine \(n\) from the equality \[ \sqrt[3]{2+11\sqrt{-1}}=2+n\sqrt{-1} \] or, after raising to the third power, \[ 2+11\sqrt{-1}=(2+n\sqrt{-1})^3, \] which means that I must have the rules of addition and multiplication of numbers that involve \(\sqrt{-1}\). For addition it is quite (only?) natural to require \[ (a+b\sqrt{-1})+(c+d\sqrt{-1})=(a+c)+(b+d)\sqrt{-1}. \] Multiplication is certainly trickier, and I choose \[ (a+b\sqrt{-1})(c+d\sqrt{-1})=(ac+bd\sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1})+(bc+ad)\sqrt{-1}=(ac-bd)+(bc+ad)\sqrt{-1}, \] where I, following Bombelli, used a “natural” calculation \(\sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1}=\sqrt{(-1)^2}=-1\), which by no means obvious (as late as 1770 Leonard Euler, great Swiss mathematician, argued that \(\sqrt{-3}\sqrt{-2}=\sqrt{6}\), which is incorrect)! But if I stick to Bombelli’s rule, I can easily infer that \(n=1\) in my formulas, i.e., \[ (2+\sqrt{-1})^3=2+11\sqrt{-1},\quad (2-\sqrt{-1})^3=2-11\sqrt{-1}, \] and everything works out as required.

Much later Euler used the symbol \(\I\) to denote the (positive) square root of \(-1\), and therefore my arithmetic rules become \begin{align*} (a+\I b)+(c+\I d)&=(a+c)+\I(b+d),\\ (a+\I b)(c+\I d)&=(ac-bd)+\I(bc+ad), \end{align*} which you all saw before.

After all these algebraic and historical details I can finally explain why the name “imaginary” was used. The main point here is that these numbers, which did not seem to have a simple physical or geometric meaning, allow us to find perfectly fine real solutions (look again at the figure with the cubic parabola), i.e., they are used as a not very well understood, but necessary intermediate step in calculations that leads to perfectly meaningful answers. Moreover, the example I picked is not an artificial one. It can be proved, using quite an involved algebraic machinery, that if a cubic polynomial with integer coefficients has three irrational real roots, it is simply impossible to express them with radicals of any degree without introducing square roots of negative numbers (this phenomenon has a poetic Latin name casus irreducibilis). That is why these unavoidable strange numbers were perceived as something mysterious (e.g., in 1702 Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, one of the fathers of Calculus, described \(\I\) as “that amphibian between existence and nonexistence.”) Only by the very end of the eighteenth century it was realized (by several people simultaneously) that there was a perfectly fine concrete interpretation of complex numbers: viz., they are exactly points (or vectors) on the plane that can be added and multiplied following some certain natural rules. I will expand on this much more in the next lecture.

1.3A digression: How to guess the roots of polynomials with integer coefficients

Any (well, almost) student knows how to solve the quadratic equation \[ ax^2+bx+c=0, \] and almost no one remembers Cardano’s formula for determining the roots of a cubic polynomial (and for a good reason, since even in simple cases it often leads to expressions with square and cubic roots that are difficult to comprehend, see one exercise below). To complicate the matter further, I would like to state that although for polynomials of degree 4 it is possible to find a formula similar to Cardano’s one (if you are curious, see Section 1.4), for a general polynomial of degree five it is impossible to find such a formula, which is a deep and beautiful mathematical result (google Abel–Ruffini theorem). So what if one needs to determine roots of a polynomial of degree bigger then two? In general this must be done numerically, and there exist very efficient algorithms of doing this. In class, however, a student is often given a polynomial equation with integer coefficients: \[ a_n x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\ldots+a_1 x+a_0=0, \] where all \(a_j\) are integers. For such equations the following holds:

Proposition 1.1. Assume that all coefficients are integers and \(a_0,a_n\neq 0\). If \(x=p/q\) is a rational root of the above equation, and \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime, then \(p\) divides \(a_0\) and \(q\) divides \(a_n\).

Hence, if asked to find a root of a polynomial with integer coefficients, I first decide which integers divide \(a_0\), which divide \(a_n\), form all possible fractions out of my lists, and test them one by one, hoping for the best.

Proof. In my proof I will use the fact that if integer \(a\) divides the product \(bc\) of integers \(b\) and \(c\), and \(a\) and \(b\) are coprime, which means that they have no common prime factors in their prime factorization, then \(a\) must divide \(c\) (this fact sometimes called Euclid’s lemma, and it certainly requires a proof on its own).

Assume that \(x=p/q\) is a rational root and \(p,q\) are coprime. Plugging it into the equation yields \[ a_n\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^n+a_{n-1}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{n-1}+\ldots+a_1\frac{p}{q}+a_0=0, \] or, after some rearrangement, \[ -a_n p^n=a_{n-1}p^{n-1}q+\ldots+a_1 p q^{n-1}+a_0 q^n. \] Here \(q\) clearly divides the right side, therefore it must divide the left side of equality as well. Since \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime then \(p^n\) and \(q\) are coprime. By Euclid’s lemma, \(q\) must divide \(a_n\).

Similarly, rearranging the equality in a different way, I get \[ -a_0q=a_nq\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^n+a_{n-1}q\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{n-1}+\ldots+a_1p. \] Now \(p\) divides the right side, hence it must divide the left side, and therefore must divide, by the same reasonings, \(a_0\). ■

Exercise 1. Show that \(p(x)=2x^3+x-1\) has no rational roots.

Exercise 2. Assume that \(a_n=1\) and formulate a useful corollary of the proven proposition.

Exercise 3. Find all roots of \[ x^3-2x+4=0 \] (there are three roots, one is real and two complex).

Using Cardano’s formula find the real root of this equation. Convince yourself that the formula gives the correct answer (this last part is not as simple as it seems).

Optional material

*1.4Solving a quartic equation or on the way to Ferrari’s formula

I mentioned above that it is possible to find a formula for the quartic equation \[ y^4+4ay^3+by^2+cy+d=0, \] where \(a,b,c,d\in\R\). Here is the main idea. Recall that while solving the quadratic equation, we first completed the square. When we were solving the cubic equation, we completed the cube. Not surprisingly, in the situation of degree four the first step would be to complete the fourth power relying on the formula \[ (A+B)^4=A^4+4A^3B+6A^2B^2+4AB^3+B^4. \] Now it is probably clear why I included coefficient 4 in the front of \(ay^3\) term above. So, I rewrite my equation as \[ y^4+4ay^3+6a^2y^2+4a^3y+a^4-6a^2y^2-4a^3y-a^4+by^2+cy+d=0 \] and hence obtain \[ (y+a)^4+y^2(b-6a^2)+y(c-4a^3)+d-a^4=0. \] At this point it should be clear that after the substitution \(x=y+a\) I will have the equation \[ x^4+2px^2+2qx+r=0, \] where my new coefficients \(p,q,r\) depend on \(a,b,c,d\). Almost immediate is to try to complete the square: \[ (x^2+p)^2+2qx+r-p^2=0, \] or, better for the following, \[ (x^2+p)^2=p^2-2qx-r. \] If I had a number on the right, the problem would be solved, but it still involves \(x\). Another good situation would be if the right hand side of the equality would be a full square, but as it is given now, I do not have enough degrees of freedom to turn it into a full square. Here comes another magical trick: let me add a number \(t\) inside the square on the left side such that adding the same amount to the right side would turn it into a full square. Not an obvious move for sure.

So, I take, instead of \((x^2+p)^2\), \((x^2+p+t)^2\), where \(t\) is a number that will be determined later. I have, \[ (x^2+p+t)^2=(x^2+p)^2+2t(x^2+p)+t^2, \] so to equal things out, I need \[ (x^2+p+t)^2=p^2-2qx-r+2t(x^2+p)+t^2, \] or, after some simplification, \[ (x^2+p+t)^2=2tx^2-2qx+(t^2+2pt+p^2-r). \] Notice that on the right I have a quadratic polynomial with respect to \(x\), and it will be a full square if and only if it has unique real root \(\hat x\) (if you are confused at this point think about \(x^2-2x+1=(x-1)^2\), which has only one real root \(\hat x=1\)). Moreover, we know that this situation exactly corresponds to the case when the discriminant \(\Delta\) of this polynomial is equal to zero. I compute \[ \Delta = 4q^2-4(2t)(t^2+2pt+p^2-r)=0\Longleftrightarrow -2t^3-4pt^2-2t(p^2-r)+q^2=0, \] but the last equation is a cubic one, which we know how to solve! We have Cardano’s formula \(\eqref{eq1:2}\), which, at least in principle, can handle any cubic equation. Let me denote this one special \(t\) that solves the last equation as \(\hat t\). For this special \(\hat t\) I can find the unique real root of \(2\hat tx^2-2qx+(\hat t^2+2p\hat t+p^2-r)=0\), which I denote \(\hat x\). Now my equation becomes \[ \bigl(x^2+p+\hat t\bigr)^2=2\hat t(x-\hat x)^2, \] or \[ x^2+p+\hat t=\pm\sqrt{2\hat t}(x-\hat x), \] which is a pair of quadratic equations, and which are easy to solve. Putting all the steps together in one formula would result in so-called Ferrari’s formula for solving quartic equations (I will not ask to do it even as an optional exercise, if you are really eager to pursue it, do it at your own risk).

Exercise 4. Using the procedure outlined above confirm that the four roots of \[ x^4+6x^2+8x+21=0 \] are \(1\pm\I\sqrt{6}\) and \(-1\pm\I\sqrt{2}\).

To summarize, while solving the cubic equation, we needed to solve a quadratic one as an intermediate step. For the quartic equation, an axillary cubic equation had to be solved. A naive intuition at this point may whisper that it is quite probable that for solving an equation of degree five we just need to be smart enough to find a correct intermediate quartic equation, and both Abel and Ruffini could be wrong. It will not work, do not try it (but if you do try hard enough you will find that an equation of degree five requires solving an equation of degree six in the middle, and the equation of degree six requires solving an equation of degree seven, and so on ad infinitum).

Exercise 5. Memorizing both Cardano’s and Ferrari’s formulas is pointless, a good idea is to remember about their existence. I did use a formula in the proofs above, which you should know in the general form. Specifically, let \(n\) be a positive integer and \(A\) and \(B\) be two real numbers. Then I claim that \[ (A+B)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n {n\choose k}A^{n-k}B^k, \] where \[ {n\choose k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}={n\choose n-k}\,, \] (and \(k!\), of course, is, \(1\cdot2\cdot\ldots\cdot k\), \(0!=1\)). This formula is called Newton’s binomial theorem. Convince yourself that it gives exactly the expressions I used above for \(n=2,3,4\). If you never saw it before, prove it. This is a good exercise.
